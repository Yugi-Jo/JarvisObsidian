# Ehlers-Danlos Syndrome Type 3 (hypermobility)

Confirmed by Joel, 10 Nov 2026. hEDS — the hypermobility type. Already assumed in every
task that touches energy, pain or mobility; this note exists so the detail is in one place
rather than inferred each time.

## Why it matters across the board

hEDS brings generalised joint hypermobility, joint instability, chronic pain that is often
neuropathic, soft and hyperextensible skin, easy bruising, and fatigue. Practical
consequences here:

- **Tasks must stay low-effort.** This is the reason behind the effort rule in
  [[Index|About Me]] and [[02 - Preferences/Index|Preferences]].
- **Musculoskeletal injury is common and can happen with minimal trauma.** Pushing through
  pain isn't a neutral act.
- **Pain is real, not explained away by weight.** hEDS patients routinely get told their
  symptoms are just their size when there's a structural problem underneath.

## The feet, specifically

Foot and ankle pain is one of the most common hEDS complaints. One foot and ankle surgical
review put it at about 78% of hEDS patients. The mechanism:

- Severe pes planus (flat feet) is the most common foot deformity in hEDS, driven by
  ligamentous laxity rather than by weight alone.
- That loads the posterior tibial tendon, which is the tendon holding the arch. Its own NHS
  risk list names hypermobility, being overweight and existing flat feet.
- Plantar fasciitis in hypermobile people tends to run worse than usual.
- Heel fat pad atrophy is more common in heavier patients and is often misdiagnosed as
  plantar fasciitis.
- GeneReviews lists bilateral piezogenic heel papules (fat herniating through the skin on
  standing) as a recognised hEDS feature.

**The important exception:** standard foot advice is built for non-hypermobile feet. Calf
and plantar fascia stretching, and rolling the arch, assume tissue that needs lengthening.
In hEDS the tissue is already lax, so aggressive stretching can make things worse.
Orthotics should support and limit movement, not push the foot into a shape it won't hold.

## Joel's foot pain, noted 10 Nov 2026

Excruciating pain and discomfort within a couple of minutes of standing. Threshold that
fast doesn't fit load alone.

**The pattern that matters, added later the same day.** On waking, before walking, the feet
are mild or not painful at all. The pain builds once he's on them. That shape argues against
classic plantar fasciitis and toward heel fat pad syndrome:

- NHS Lanarkshire on fat pad syndrome: with the first steps in the morning and after rest,
  "the discomfort is not as painful, the pain tends to build with increased weight bearing
  activity." Plantar fasciopathy is the other way round: worst on the first steps after rest.
- A scoping review on heel fat pad syndrome found morning first-step pain *substantially
  decreased* the likelihood of it (odds ratios 0.04 to 0.07), while worsening pain with
  prolonged standing, night pain and pain in both feet *greatly increased* it (odds ratios
  20.9 to 25.0).

Mechanism: lying down takes all load off the heel, so a thinned pad isn't being compressed
onto the heel bone. Standing puts his full body mass straight through it, which is why the
pain tracks load rather than time. Both conditions can coexist, and plantar fasciitis makes
the pad wear faster because it stops the foot distributing weight.

**Why this steers the treatment.** Fat pad responds to cushioning and offloading, which makes
the steroid injection route worse than it already looked: repeated injections cause the very
fat pad atrophy that's likely driving this.

Open: GP appointment, then a podiatry referral. Ask about plantar fasciitis, fat pad atrophy
and posterior tibial tendon dysfunction, and make sure whoever assesses it knows about the
hEDS so the advice isn't the generic kind.

## Appointment brief

Take these in, in these words.

- **Ask for:** a podiatry referral. Say the words heel fat pad atrophy, plantar fasciitis and
  posterior tibial tendon dysfunction.
- **Say the pattern:** pain is mild or absent on the first steps in the morning, and builds
  with standing. Ask them to scan the heel fat pad rather than assume plantar fasciitis.
- **Say the EDS out loud.** It changes the orthotic brief and rules out the standard
  stretching advice.
- **Push back on:** calf and plantar fascia stretching, rolling the arch on a frozen bottle,
  and a steroid injection as the first move.
- **Ask about:** whether the autonomic and mast cell side has ever been assessed, since
  treating those reduces pain and painkiller need.
- **Same-day instead of waiting** if a foot turns red, hot or swollen, changes shape, goes
  cold or numb, or a blister or wound won't heal. Those point at infection, poor circulation
  or Charcot foot.

## Relevant to the mobility thread

The chair choice is affected by this. EDS makes sitting posture, pressure distribution and
transfers harder than they look on paper, so footplates, leg rests and seat width matter more
than they would for someone without it. See [[To Do]] and [[Carbon Fibre Wearable Chair]].
